# From Rule Matching to Embodied Closed Loops: Where I Stopped a Decade of Technical Exploration

I wrote the first line of code in the winter of 2015. More than ten years have passed. The project changed names several times, from A.B.C. to A.R.I.E.L. and back to K.R.I.S.T.Y., before settling into the unfinished state of v5.0. This post is not a celebration of results. It is an attempt to walk the entire path from the beginning, explaining what each stage actually did, why it was designed that way, why it later felt insufficient, and why I finally decided to stop.

Many people treat L.A.K.E.S., A.L.L.E.S., and H.A.R.P.E.R. as modules inside the main project. They are not. They were independent technical explorations I ran around 2017 during the A.R.I.E.L. period. Their only purpose was to test whether certain hypotheses could actually run on the hardware and frameworks available at the time. None of them were ever formally connected to the main product line. They remain in the repository only as experimental records.

The whole series began in a very plain way.

## Early Beginnings: Advance Bot for Chatting (A.B.C.) v1.0 (Late 2015–2016)

From late 2015 to 2016 I built A.B.C. v1.0. I simply wanted a completely offline chatbot that started in a few milliseconds and depended on no external models. The core was a collection of regular expressions plus an extremely minimal `SessionContext` dictionary. User input arrived, named capture groups extracted a name or location, and those variables were filled into pre-written response templates. If a template contained a callable function, the system computed the current time or performed simple logic on the spot. Everything ran on either a Tkinter GUI or Flask. Memory use stayed low and crashes were rare.

Advantages were obvious: there was no dependency hell and no model-loading delay. Any input that matched a rule produced a reply.

Disadvantages were equally clear: a slightly inverted sentence, a synonym, or an extra space sent the system straight into the fallback chit-chat mode. Worse, a single global `SessionContext` instance meant concurrent users overwrote one another’s context. At the time I considered it good enough, because the original goal was only “it runs, it demonstrates, it needs no network.”

## Symbolic Calculation: Knowledge Retrieval and Inference System for Test Yielding (K.R.I.S.T.Y.) v1.0(2016–2017)

Next came K.R.I.S.T.Y. v1.0 between 2016 and 2017. This time the problem was product pricing and tax calculation. Data loaded from SQLite was turned into a NetworkX directed graph. Product nodes linked to a tax-rate node through `subject_to` edges. Incoming queries were tokenized with NLTK, the matching node was located, and if the question involved a total price the system wrote the symbolic expression `p*(1+t)` in SymPy and substituted the numbers. The result was an amount accurate to two decimal places, free of floating-point error and free of model hallucination.

The real value of this system was the demonstration that certain problems should never be forced through a neural network. Tax calculation is deterministic; symbolic algebra is simply more reliable than any trained model. Natural-language understanding, however, remained primitive, little more than substring containment. Adding a new product type still required code changes; there was no dynamic schema. I already felt that pure rules plus graph structure could solve precise calculation yet could not solve genuine semantic understanding.

## Introducing Neural Networks: Advanced Retrieval and Inference Engine for Learning (A.R.I.E.L.) v2.0 (2017)

A.R.I.E.L. v2.0, begun in 2017, was the first time a neural network entered the main line. The architecture was a bidirectional GRU encoder, a unidirectional GRU decoder, and a Rational Gate in between. The gate fed the encoder’s final time-step hidden state into a two-layer MLP and produced a hallucination-risk score between 0 and 1. Anything above 0.6 triggered an immediate refusal. The knowledge base lived in SQLite tables for entities and facts; longest N-gram matching extracted entities and assembled them into context. A Seq2Seq grammar-correction module sat in front; Redis caching and Celery asynchronous tasks sat behind.

This was my first serious attempt to confront the possibility that a model might simply invent answers. The Rational Gate idea was straightforward, yet in practice it only inspected the final time step and remained almost blind to conflicts earlier in a long sequence. The vocabulary was built by frequency truncation, so out-of-vocabulary tokens collapsed into `<unk>`. Decoding was pure greedy search and therefore prone to repetition. Concurrent writes to SQLite frequently produced lock errors. Still, the system was an early effort to join deterministic knowledge with generative models, what later became known as RAG in embryonic form.

## Parallel Explorations (Around 2017)

In the same period I ran three separate explorations: L.A.K.E.S., A.L.L.E.S., and H.A.R.P.E.R.

### Linguistic Analysis and Knowledge Extraction System: Unstructured Text to Structured Knowledge (L.A.K.E.S.)

L.A.K.E.S. had a single clear goal: turn unstructured text into structured knowledge. The pipeline first used spaCy for fast token-level analysis, then Stanford CoreNLP for coreference, OpenIE, and constituency parsing. Long documents were automatically chunked; cross-chunk coreference clusters were merged by the lowercase text of the representative mention. Distant supervision relied on an inverted index plus Jaccard similarity at threshold 0.75 to produce BIO labels. Named-entity recognition used a BiLSTM-CRF, relation extraction used a Position-Aware Relation Extractor, and event extraction used a JointEventExtractor that predicted triggers and roles together.

I implemented the full Viterbi algorithm and forward algorithm by hand; the transition matrix hard-coded illegal START and STOP transitions. Position embeddings were dual relative positions; attention was a simple linear layer followed by masked softmax. The linguistic features were highly interpretable and the weak-supervision mechanism suited low-resource domains. Yet the stack depended on 2017 packages, torch 0.3.1 `Variable` syntax is essentially unrunnable today. CoreNLP ran as a single-threaded HTTP server and deadlocked under multi-threading. Memory-leak risk was high. Jaccard proved almost useless for long-tail relations. These were the reasons I never connected L.A.K.E.S. to the main line.

### Artificial Linguistic Learning and Emulation System: Controllable Style, Translation, and Memory (A.L.L.E.S.)

A.L.L.E.S. aimed at controllable style transfer, cross-lingual translation, and multi-turn memory, while deliberately avoiding Transformers. The encoder was a bidirectional LSTM; the decoder concatenated word embeddings, language embeddings, style embeddings, attention context, and memory context. Style was decoupled by a Gradient Reversal Layer whose forward pass was identity and whose backward pass multiplied by –α. Memory lived in an external `PersistentMemoryBank` that kept at most five turns of hidden states and forced a detach-to-CPU on every update so the computation graph was cut. Catastrophic forgetting was addressed with Elastic Weight Consolidation: Fisher Information was estimated on the first twenty batches of each epoch and a quadratic penalty with λ = 5000 was added. Data loading used memory-mapped files with a lazy offset table to avoid file-descriptor leaks under multi-process workers.

At the time the design looked complete. Style and language conditions were genuinely controllable, and EWC existed at the formula level. Pure LSTM, however, remained limited on long sequences; even curriculum learning that gradually raised max length to 120 could not match later Transformers. The memory bank exhausted GPU memory on large datasets. Poor α schedules produced mode collapse or NaNs. The BLEU calculation was only crude token overlap. What I carried away was the necessity of external memory that safely severs the computation graph, and the usefulness of adversarial style decoupling. The overall architecture had already been overtaken by the times.

### Humane Analysis of Rhetoric, Pronunciation, and Expression in Response: End-to-End Text-to-Speech Dialogue (H.A.R.P.E.R.)

H.A.R.P.E.R. went further, attempting an end-to-end text-to-speech dialogue system. Text generation offered both a standard Seq2Seq path and a hierarchical HRED path whose utterance encoder fed a ContextRNN. Style conditioning used tone embeddings together with a RhetoricAnalyzer that combined rule-based detection of anaphora, chiasmus, and the like with an optional BiLSTM. Optimization used policy-gradient RLHF; the reward was the average of a WGAN score and a style-classifier probability, with a moving-average baseline. Speech synthesis followed a Tacotron-style ProsodyAcousticDecoder with location-sensitive attention and a CBHG post-net, ending in Griffin-Lim phase reconstruction.

In 2017 the ability to implement full RLHF, WGAN-GP, location-sensitive attention, and CBHG already counted as advanced. Griffin-Lim nevertheless produced metallic audio even after fifty iterations. The entropy coefficient of 0.01 made RLHF collapse easily. The HRED and Seq2Seq paths were only demonstrated separately in the main script and never truly fused. Rhetoric rules generated frequent false positives. These three explorations confirmed several concrete lessons: memory must be externalized and safely detached, style can be decoupled adversarially, and the vocoders of that era were still inadequate. They completed a closed-loop verification of “understand → generate → express,” yet none of them entered the product line.

## Maturing the RAG Pipeline: K.R.I.S.T.Y. v3.0 (2019–2024)

Returning to the main line, K.R.I.S.T.Y. v3.0 (2019–2024) made the RAG pipeline more complete. spaCy extracted entities, BM25 retrieved the top-k passages from an `index.jsonl` file, and a word-co-occurrence graph was built on the retrieved context with PyTorch Geometric. BERT produced last-hidden and pooled outputs; several GCN layers extracted graph features that were residual-added back into the BERT hidden states. A Reasoning Gate computed a logic score; any score above 0.7 triggered an immediate hard refusal. Safe generations passed through T5 and a custom LogitWarper that dynamically suppressed uncertain tokens at every decoding step according to the current logic score.

This was the first system that combined a hard threshold gate before generation with soft logit warping during decoding. Multi-task training also included early stopping. The graph, however, used random Gaussian node features and only window-2 co-occurrence edges, so the theoretical advantage of GCN was largely diluted. Loading both BERT and T5 doubled memory demand. I still believed residual injection of graph features into a Transformer was a valuable direction, yet the implementation details remained too crude.

## Multimodality: K.R.I.S.T.Y. v4.0 (2020–2025)

Version 4.0 (2020–2025) began true multimodality. A single forward pass produced text, 46-DoF motion, and a 64×64 image. The knowledge graph used a Watts–Strogatz small-world topology; GCN features were routed into a shared latent vector `z`. FiLM layers allowed `z` to modulate both the DanceDecoder (GRU) and the ImageDecoder (transposed convolutions). Losses were balanced automatically by Kendall’s learnable uncertainty weights across five heterogeneous tasks. Motion exceeding a velocity threshold received exponential smoothing; images whose standard deviation fell too low were forcibly sharpened by a Laplacian kernel.

One-prompt multimodal generation was uncommon at the time, and the WebUI could even render the skeleton live. Resolution of 64×64 was nevertheless too low, transposed convolutions produced checkerboard artifacts, and the motion lacked any physical constraint, so feet slid or bodies sank through the floor. I had already begun to feel the difference between “looks good” and “is usable.”

## Physical AI and Embodiment: K.R.I.S.T.Y. v5.0

The final version, v5.0, aimed directly at physical AI. A `PersistentGraphStore` performed multi-hop BFS over SQLite. An `MoEKinematicsRouter` sent the latent vector to a RoboticsExpert (joint angles hard-clamped to ±0.5 rad), a DanceExpert (dynamic range scaled by 1.2), or a MusicExpert. Generated trajectories were injected into PyBullet under `POSITION_CONTROL`; tracking error and base-tipping penalties were computed, and a differentiable proxy could refine the latent vector by gradient when needed. Multi-robot collision avoidance used artificial potential fields in joint space. An `AgentGraphSupervisor` drove the state-machine pipeline. Outputs could be published at 30 Hz to a ROS 2 `/joint_commands` topic, exported as BVH, or converted to ONNX.

This was the most complete closed loop of the entire line, from natural language through GraphRAG, MoE kinematics, physical validation, and hardware commands. The path existed. Yet high-frequency `stepSimulation` on CPU introduced noticeable latency, and the dependency stack that now included PyTorch, PyBullet, ROS 2, Three.js and more made maintenance cost exceed any reasonable return. Development stopped here because the marginal benefit of continuing had become too low.

## Lines of Evolution Across the Decade

Looking across the whole trajectory, several clear lines of evolution appear.

### Knowledge Representation

Knowledge representation moved from hard-coded regular expressions to SQLite key-value pairs, then to static NetworkX graphs, then to word-co-occurrence PyG graphs, then to small-world GCN routing, and finally to persistent multi-hop GraphRAG. Each layer solved the generalization weakness of the previous one, yet each also introduced new complexity and computational cost.

### Safety and Hallucination Control

Safety and hallucination control began with nothing, progressed to a Rational Gate that inspected only the final hidden state, then to symbolic algebra that guaranteed zero numerical error, then to a hard gate before generation plus logit warping during decoding, then to regular-expression filtering plus zero-shot classification, and finally to physical feasibility checks, potential-field collision avoidance, and an agentic circuit-breaker. More layers of protection were added, yet every layer assumed the representation produced by the previous layer was trustworthy, an assumption that frequently fails in real settings.

### Output Modalities

Output modalities expanded from pure text, to text plus retrieved facts, to symbolic calculation results, to filtered text, to text plus motion plus image, and finally to text plus motion plus image plus BVH plus ROS 2 commands. Each new modality required extra smoothing, sharpening, physical alignment, or hardware interfaces, and the system’s points of fragility grew in parallel.

## Practical Lessons from Implementation

What I actually learned from these projects was rarely the elegant formula. It was the practical failures encountered while implementing them.

- External memory must be detached to CPU; otherwise the computation graph grows without bound.
- A gradient-reversal layer looks simple, yet a poorly scheduled α collapses an entire training run.
- Distant-supervision thresholds set too high miss long-tail cases; thresholds set too low inject massive noise.
- Symbolic calculation remains more reliable than any neural network on deterministic problems.
- Multi-task losses without learnable uncertainty weights are easily dominated by a single term.
- A physics-engine closed loop looks impressive until latency and stability problems appear at once.
- Once a stack of heavy frameworks is containerized, Dockerfile maintenance cost exceeds the cost of the algorithms themselves.

## Why I Stopped

More important still, I gradually confirmed that “forward-looking” has two different time scales. Relative to the outside world, implementing complete RLHF plus Tacotron in 2017, or a language-to-ROS-2 path in 2025, still counted as advanced. Relative to my own standard, once I could already see the next layer of bottlenecks and judged that those bottlenecks could not be solved elegantly inside the existing architecture, continuing to pile on features merely prolonged the life of a design that had already become obsolete.

Version 5.0 remains unfinished not because it could not be completed, but because further work would only have reproduced the same problems in more complicated form. Additional experts could be added to the MoE, potential-field parameters could be retuned, ONNX export could be further optimized, yet the core representation capacity, the computational cost of physical alignment, and the guarantee of cross-modal consistency showed no fundamental advance. The world continues to move, larger models after the Transformer, better vocoders, more mature sim-to-real toolchains. My own criterion is stricter: “it still runs” is no longer enough. A design must retain structural advantage in the next wave of technology before it deserves further investment.

I therefore marked the repository deprecated and stopped maintenance. The code remains, the documents remain, the experimental records remain. They document a complete path from rules to symbols, from symbols to neural networks, from neural networks to multimodality, and from multimodality to embodiment. Along that path lie many designs that once felt clever and later felt clumsy. Those clumsy places are precisely what made the next place to apply effort clearer.

Ten years is long enough. The time that remains will be given to problems that still appear to contain structural room.
